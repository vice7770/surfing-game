## Read this before judging them

**It will look dirty at first glance, and that reaction is correct.** Real whitewater is not white: an active
whitecap reflects about **0.50**, thin foam **0.18**, and never more than about 0.55–0.60 even in a laboratory
(Dierssen 2019; Koepke 1984), where ours was a diffuse albedo of **0.840**. In a side-on view whitewater is the
**darker** thing in the frame.

**So judge foam against water inside one picture, never the foam on its own.** Absolute brightness depends on an
exposure nobody knows; the ratio inside one frame does not.

## What is right, what is not, and what will probably annoy you

**Right:** the broken mass is no longer a flat white sheet, the wave's body reads thicker against it, and the lace's
own network is visible where it used to be white on white. On `bore-face` the whitewater comes within 2 % of the water
beside it (1.021) — level with it, not yet darker.

**Not right, and it is the honest half:** 1.102 is not 0.748. The flat water in our frame is **0.80×** the
photograph's, and the region's large-scale light and shade is still about half the photograph's, even though the
small-scale relief now matches.

**The likeliest objection:** the **aged lace far behind the break**. It is the oldest foam in the frame, so it takes
the full 0.18, and over the pool's bright bed it can read as a sandy or dirty stain. If that is the thing that looks
wrong, say so — the number to move is the aged end, and `../c2-step5i.md` §10 item 2 is the principled way to move
it, from the bubble layers the aeration field already carries rather than from a constant.

**Not shown here:** motion. Every frame is one held still, and how the aged lace reads as it drifts is exactly what a
still cannot say.
