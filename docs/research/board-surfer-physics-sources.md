# Board and surfer physics: source audit

Research for a proposed board–surfer model to work with the Breakline wave-formation plan. The three user-supplied publications are identified below. The two reviews describe the evidence landscape; mechanical parameters and acceptance criteria should be grounded in their cited primary experiments or simulations, then calibrated in the game. All findings here describe the cited study's conditions, not universal surfing constants.

## Primary evidence relevant to simulation

### Catch and paddling

- [Kimura and Kakinuma, *Journal of Japan Society of Civil Engineers* 2015](https://doi.org/10.2208/kaigan.71.I_61) used breaking-wave numerical simulations to derive two takeoff requirements: enough **horizontal paddling velocity** to avoid the peak overtaking the surfer, and a **position on the face** where the along-front force acts downward when paddling stops. They evaluated both spilling and plunging waves. This is a model result, not a measured threshold valid for every sea state.
- [Sheppard et al., *International Journal of Sports Science & Coaching* 2013](https://doi.org/10.1260/1747-9541.8.1.43) compared technique changes in 15 m sprint paddles by competitive male surfers. Lower chest position and lower arm recovery improved sprint performance in their protocol; reach variation had no significant effect. This can inform prone animation and input efficiency, while speed should still emerge from paddle impulses and water drag.
- [Sheppard et al., *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22290522/) measured ten competitive male surfers and found relative upper-body pulling strength associated with 5–15 m sprint paddling time and peak velocity. Association does not justify a fixed propulsion force.

### Hull, rails, and fins

- [Oggiano and Pierella, *Proceedings* 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/proceedings2060309) compared three surfboard shapes with unsteady free-surface CFD. Their rocker variation changed lift, drag, and maneuver forces; the tested tail-shape variation had little effect in those conditions. The authors model the hull as a planing surface and describe paddling followed by acceleration down the wave face. This supports separate displacement/buoyancy and speed-dependent planing contributions, but their CFD was not a complete human–board–breaking-wave validation.
- [D’Ambrosio, *Proceedings* 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/proceedings2020049068) computed lift, drag, and pitch moment over board attitude to estimate a center of pressure and static equilibrium under a surrogate surfer weight. It supports computing **force application points and moments**, rather than steering the board by directly rotating it. These are CFD equilibrium maps, not a validated live-surf controller.
- [Falk et al., *Journal of Fluids and Structures* 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfluidstructs.2019.07.006) simulated a three-fin setup at 5 m/s and 0–45° attack. Fin lift produces turning moments; drag grows with attack, and their unsteady simulations found flow separation and vortex shedding above roughly 20° with fluctuating forces. Their board was simplified to a flat wall and omitted the free surface and surfer, so **20° is not a calibrated in-game universal stall angle**.
- [Kniesburges et al., *Scientific Reports* 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-94834-0) instrumented one fin and recorded pressure during turns on an artificial river wave after tank validation. The opposite fin faces showed a pressure difference consistent with lateral lift directed outward from the board. This is direct in-water evidence for fin side force, but one fin, one wave site, and assumptions about pressure distribution limit absolute force calibration.
- [Lahlou, Florida Institute of Technology thesis](https://repository.fit.edu/etd/1164/) reports theoretical, experimental, and computational surfboard drag comparisons. It can be useful for a later hull-drag calibration pass; check its exact tow conditions before importing coefficients.

### Stance, turns, and loss of balance

- [Shormann and in het Panhuis, *PLOS ONE* 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0232035) instrumented shortboards with GPS and inertial sensors during ocean-wave surfing while comparing fin configurations. Their Table 1 reports one intermediate rider at **73 kg** on a **178 × 47 × 6.0 cm, 25.9 L** three-fin shortboard; other participants used larger boards. Their maneuver records include board yaw, pitch, roll, speed and bottom-turn/cutback duration. This offers a coherent **candidate reference setup** and field comparison variables, not universal board dimensions or force coefficients. Board mass was not reported for that setup; fin-treatment comparisons and differing wave conditions limit any direct calibration of the game's board forces.
- [Borgonovo-Santos et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/s21051783) directly observed the pop-up landing's front/rear load asymmetry. It provides a landing impulse target and foot placement estimate. It **does not establish** a 72/28 load split during trimming or turning.
- [Lestrade et al., ISBS 2015 proceedings](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/6478) built a board with a synchronized force platform and inertial sensor, then tested it on surfed waves. This demonstrates a practical validation pair: foot force/center of pressure and board acceleration/orientation. The published abstract does not provide universal controller coefficients.
- [Whitting et al., *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14755) analyzed video of 199 frontside bottom turns in finals of one professional event. Their 47-feature checklist found turn-exit features associated with the next cutback or top turn. This supports distinct turn phases and visible body preparation, but does not directly measure hydrodynamic forces or prove those cues cause a turn.
- [Bruton et al., *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0031512517717853) studied 42 participants in a **laboratory 60 cm drop landing**. Greater knee and ankle motion range was associated with greater surfing experience. This supports a compliant knee/ankle landing pose and recovery, but it is not an on-water fall model.

## Modeling consequences, explicitly provisional

The following are **simulation choices inferred from the evidence**, not measured constants:

1. Represent the board as a rigid body with mass, inertia, hull contact points, and separate rail/fin contact points. Query the same water height, normal, and local velocity used by the wave solver. At each wet point, apply hydrostatic support plus a bounded speed-dependent planing force and drag in the **water-relative** direction. Sum forces and moments about the board center; avoid direct positional wave locking. Oggiano and Pierella support the two support regimes; D’Ambrosio supports force and center-of-pressure accounting.
2. Compute fin side force from water-relative speed and attack, with a low-attack lift region and smooth high-attack saturation plus rising drag. Permit rail immersion to add a lateral force and roll coupling. Falk et al. motivate the shape, and Kniesburges et al. confirm lateral fin pressure in one ridden wave. Coefficients, stall curve, and rail contribution require game calibration.
3. Give the surfer an articulated or reduced center-of-mass body linked to two foot contacts after pop-up. Foot placement, load transfer, ankle/knee compliance, and lean shift the combined center of mass and apply torque to the board. Use measured pop-up timing/landing asymmetry for a **starting animation and impulse envelope only**. Standing load split, control response time, balance thresholds, and protective reactions remain design parameters.
4. Resolve catch from board velocity **relative to the local crest**, board position on the sloping face, and maintained water support. Do not trigger a catch by a timer or a single world-coordinate band. Kimura and Kakinuma justify the horizontal/vertical criteria; exact inequalities must be tested against the game's wave field and scaling.
5. If foot contact fails or a water/lip impulse exceeds recoverable posture, detach the surfer dynamically. Continue gravity, water-relative drag, and buoyancy for the body; allow the board to keep its own water dynamics. This is a game-physics extension: the cited surf biomechanics studies do not determine human-in-breaking-water drag or buoyancy coefficients.

## Validation targets and unresolved calibration

- **Source-grounded checks:** dry pop-up lasts near the measured ~1.2 s reference and changes from hand support to two foot contacts; catch depends on relative velocity and face position; fin loading changes sign with turn direction; rocker/trim and speed affect hull support and drag; force application points produce pitch/roll/yaw moments.
- **Game-specific checks:** the default wave remains catchable and rideable for an extended session without automatic translation; steering creates controllable bottom and top turns; excessive attack or lip impact can cause a repeatable wipeout; detached surfer contacts and floats in the water while the board continues separately. Test across seeds and wave settings, record force budgets and contact state, and tune only after these observables are exposed.
- **Known evidence gap:** the supplied reviews explicitly note sparse field measurements of turning and live wave interaction. Do not present tuned gameplay coefficients as empirically measured surfing constants.
