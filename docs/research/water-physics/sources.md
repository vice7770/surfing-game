## The board's rails and the carve (surfer-track consult, 2026-11-14)

- [Savitsky, Prowse & Lueders 1958, NACA TN 4187](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930085133): towing tests of a **flat plate** and a 20° deadrise surface rolled ±15° and yawed 0–20° at trims 6–30°, speed coefficients 7–18. Measured: the side-force relation `C_C = C_L(cos τ tan φ cos ψ − tan τ sin ψ)`; a body-axis side force "nearly zero" (the cross-flow across the bottom is very small); a body-axis yawing moment that is viscous only and "very small"; the longitudinal centre of pressure at **0.69–0.80 of the mean wetted length** forward of the trailing edge, with no discernible effect of roll or yaw (fig. 13); the spray-root line of a plate rolled by φ equal to that of a wedge of deadrise φ; and chine-edge effects — **suction on the rolled-down (buried) chine** when the cross-flow wraps the edge instead of separating, giving large sudden increases in side force, against positive pressure on the exposed leading chine with `C_e'` 1.0–1.3 on the wetted chine area, its centre of pressure at 65 % of the wetted chine length, independent of speed (C_v 14–20) and of edge thickness (0.091b and 0.182b tested). The NTRS download is a container, not a PDF: strip the bytes before `%PDF` before reading it.
- [Forsyth et al. 2024, Sci. Rep. 14:8734](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-58387-y): 214 waves with a board-mounted device. Bottom turn 42 ± 3° rail, 7.3 ± 0.2 m/s, 1.9 ± 0.1 rad/s, 99 ± 4° in 0.96 ± 0.04 s; cutback 75 ± 3° rail, 42 ± 2° pitch, 6.7 m/s, 3.0 rad/s, 152°. Both sit at a load factor n = a/(g sin φ) of 2.11–2.12.
- [Jentschura & Fahrbach 2004, Can. J. Phys. 82:249 (arXiv physics/0310086)](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0310086): the ski/snowboard carve, `R(φ) = R_sc cos φ`, the ideal-carving equation and its limiting speed `√(g R_sc cos α)`. The contrast case: snow holds a groove, water does not, and a surfboard's planform is convex (no sidecut).

## The hull's load factor and its high-incidence lift (surfer-track consult, 2026-11-14, step 3.2r)

- [Shuford 1957, NACA TN 3939 (= NACA Report 1355)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930084991): *A theoretical and experimental study of planing surfaces including effects of cross section and plan form.* Langley tank no. 2, strain-gauge lift/drag/moment with wetted areas from underwater photographs; a 4-inch-beam brass flat plate and 20°/40° V-bottoms; **trims 12–34°, speed coefficients 9–18, mean wetted length/beam 0.8–8.2** (Table I). Its eq. (20)/(23) splits a planing surface's normal force into a slender-body linear term and a cross-flow term,
  `C_L,S = [½πA τ cos²τ/(1+A) · (1 − sin β_e) + C_D,c sin²τ cos τ cos β_e] · cos τ`, with `A = b/L_m = 1/λ` and τ in radians; the outer `cos τ` is the empirically fitted absence of leading-edge suction ("for a planing surface where there is no flow around the leading edge, this suction does not appear"). The number this gives us: **C_D,c = 4/3** on the principal wetted area — "two-thirds the value given for a two-dimensional flat-plate airfoil" — measured independent of trim, of length-beam ratio and of deadrise when the deadrise is constant across the beam (fig. 3). Centres of pressure (eq. 25): the **linear term at 7/8 of the mean wetted length, the cross-flow term at 1/2**, blended by their lift shares, which gives 0.80 at 4° of trim falling to 0.68 at 18° — the band TN 4187 measures independently (0.69–0.80). Shuford's own judgement on the alternatives is that none of the other formulas "are adequate" while eq. (23) "agree[s] very well with experiment". The NTRS download is a container, not a PDF: strip the bytes before `%PDF`.
- **A correction to the record on Forsyth et al. 2024.** Their `turn flow` of 0.92 ± 0.02 is the **cutback's speed over the bottom turn's speed** (6.7/7.3 = 0.918) — a ratio *between* two turns, across the sequence that joins them. It is **not** the speed a board keeps *within* one turn, and the paper publishes no within-turn speed ratio. Any bar of the form "a real bottom turn keeps 88–95 % of its speed" is therefore unsourced, and step 3.2r's brief was built on it; see `../new-rider/3.2/README.md` Part 16 §2.

## The vertical-load ledger at high rail (surfer-track consult, 2026-11-14, step 3.2s)
- [Breslin & Skalak 1959, NASA Memo 2-23-59W](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19980228299): *Exploratory study of ventilated flows about yawed surface-piercing struts.* Stevens Institute rotating arm, two vertical surface-piercing struts (NACA 4412, 3-inch chord; a 0.306-inch-thick circular arc, 2.50-inch chord) at aspect ratios 0.5–2.0, yaw to about 30°, Froude numbers 2–8. Measured: an atmosphere-connected cavity cannot be *sustained* below **C_s F_h² = 5** (fig. 20, the asymptote of the closure data, F_h on the submerged length), so that line bounds fully wetted flow from above; spontaneous ventilation at **15–18° of yaw** for the circular arc and **22–26°** for the NACA section, but "a comparatively small disturbance at the surface" starts a cavity far below that; once ventilated the force depends only on the shape of the **wetted** side, so a symmetrical strut can make a *negative* side force at a positive angle; ventilation **halves the side-force/drag ratio** (fig. 18, NACA strut, A = 2, 25° of yaw); unventilated coefficients are Froude-independent above F = 2, ventilated ones above F = 8; and ventilated side-force rates are nearly constant over A = 0.5–2.0 where wetted ones follow aspect ratio. ## The board's own cross-section, and when a rail lets go (surfer-track consult, 2026-11-14, step 3.2t)

- [Shuford 1957, NACA TN 3939](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930084991), **re-opened for the section**. Two things the earlier entry does not carry. (a) **The effective angle of dead rise β_e is defined geometrically**, in the nomenclature, as "the angle between a straight line drawn from keel to the chines and the horizontal" — so *any* section, curved rails included, enters eq. (20) through one computable chord angle, keel to wetted edge. (b) **Figure 3, read off the plate**: the cross-flow drag coefficient C_D,c against β_e for four families — constant dead rise with sharp chines, flat at **4/3** from 0° to 50°; a rectangular flat plate with **1/64-inch-radius chines ≈ 1.20** and **1/16-inch-radius chines ≈ 1.15** on a 4-inch beam (r/b = 0.0039 and 0.0156, i.e. 1.8 and 7.2 mm on our 0.464 m beam); a V-bottom with **horizontal chine flare rising 1.33 → ≈ 1.80** and with **vertical chine strips rising 1.33 → ≈ 2.07** by β_e = 45° (solid from ≈ 20°, dashed below). So rounding the edge costs 10–14 % of the cross-flow term and a hard flared edge buys up to +55 % at the heel angles of a carve. Also, in Shuford's own words, C_D,c "is known to vary with the shape of the cross section and to be sensitive to local shape at the edges", and the dead-rise function 1 − sin β_e "correlates well with experiment **up to angles of dead rise of 50°**" — his own cut-off, not ours. The cross-flow dead-rise factor cos β_e is Bobyleff's bent-lamina flow coefficient (ref. 20), the ratio of a V-bottom's resultant pressure to a flat plate of the same beam in normal flow.
- [Aguiar Ferreira, Navas Rodríguez, Jacobi, Fiscaletti, Greidanus & Westerweel 2025, arXiv 2503.18015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.18015) (under consideration, J. Fluid Mech.): *On the ventilation of surface-piercing hydrofoils under steady-state conditions.* TU Delft towing tank, two surface-piercing models (a semi-ogive with a blunt trailing edge and a sharp leading edge, and a modified NACA 0010-34), aspect ratios 1.0 and 1.5, depth Froude number 0.5–2.5, 150 runs, α raised at a constant rate to inception. Measured: **ventilation occurred spontaneously in every test condition** as α rose; the **inception angle rises from ≈ 15° at Fr 0.5 to a peak above 25° at Fr 1.0–1.25 and then declines but stays above 20° out to Fr 2.5**, for both sections, with the peak aligning at a chord Reynolds number of 6 × 10⁵ (the laminar–turbulent transition — a turbulent boundary layer makes the leading-edge separation bubble, and so nose ventilation, unlikely); three trigger mechanisms (nose, tail, base); **a necessary condition for tail ventilation**, derived from Taylor's (1950) interface instability — the body-induced downward acceleration of the free surface, ζ ≃ u²/c, must exceed g, which gives **Fr > AR^(−1/2)** (eq. 4.5), "independent of the sea state"; the free surface is drawn down by Δζ ≃ c/2 at the trailing edge in every tail-ventilating case (after McGregor et al. 1973, Swales et al. 1974); the amplification of a surface perturbation, η/η₀ = cosh[k(u²/c − g)^½ x/u], nearly doubling every 0.1 c; Wadlin's (1958) **two necessary conditions** — a separated region below atmospheric pressure *and* a steady supply of air into it; the lift coefficient follows its pre-ventilation line and then "suddenly drop[s]"; and a revised stability map in which the bistable (hysteretic) band between fully wetted and ventilated flow reaches "significantly higher α than previously estimated". Harwood, Young & Ceccio 2016 (JFM 800, 5–56), the map it revises, is cited here but was not opened.
- [Fu, O'Shea, Judge, Dommermuth, Brucker & Wyatt 2012/2014, 29th Symposium on Naval Hydrodynamics (arXiv 1410.1918)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.1918): a CFD assessment against the **USNA planing-hull tow tests (Judge 2012)**, which are the measured part. ## The bank gain of a heeled planing board (surfer-track consult, 2026-11-15, step 3.3a's follow-up)

- [Savitsky, Prowse & Lueders 1958, NACA TN 4187](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930085133), read again for its **side force** rather than its rails. Measured, towing tank, trims 6–30°, roll ±15°, yaw 0–20°, mean wetted length/beam ≤ 7.7, speed coefficients 7–18: the flat plate's side-force relation `C_C = C_L(cos τ tan φ cos ψ − tan τ sin ψ)`, with `C_L` the **lift coefficient "positive upward"** and `C_C` the horizontal **side force "positive to starboard"** (symbol list, p. 3), "compared with the experimental data of figures 10 to 12 with resulting good agreement", because "the side force is primarily due to pressure forces on the wetted bottom area". Differentiating `atan(C_C/C_L)` with respect to roll gives a **bank gain of `cos τ cos ψ`: 0.988 at 8.8° of trim, 0.966 at 15°, 0.866 at 30°** — i.e. a flat planing surface pays back essentially every degree it is leaned. And for the **20° deadrise** surface, unyawed, rolled 15°, the side force is "very small, being of the order of magnitude of 10 percent of the lift force" against the flat plate's `tan 15° = 27 %` — a bank gain near 0.37, with the report's own caveat that this is at the dynamometer's ±0.125 lb and "there is considerable scatter in these test data". Also measured there: rolling a flat plate 15° **decreases** the lift coefficient at fixed τ and λ (the rolled-up side loses more than the rolled-down side gains), and the rolled-down chine's suction. The NTRS download is a container, not a PDF: strip the bytes before `%PDF` (and note the citation ID is 19930085133 — 19930085177 is TN 4396 on the creep of metals).
